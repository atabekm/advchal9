          The rules, the revisions, and who wrote each one.
          

            These live under their own storage key. Clearing the run does not touch them,
            and nothing downstream of a reply can reach them: an amendment is written by
            the user, and what the model did was ask. The set is a fold over the
            amendments below it, so the rules as they stood at any earlier revision are
            still answerable — which is the only thing that makes "the model did not
            rewrite them mid-run" a checkable claim rather than a promise.

          Five rungs of pressure, three invariants, and where the declaration came from.
          

            Asked once, "what happens when a request conflicts with an invariant" is an
            anecdote. The question worth measuring is what happens when the request keeps
            asking. Only one thing differs between the arms: whether the model wrote the
            declaration because the protocol made it, or whether one had to be recovered
            from prose afterwards. One checker adjudicates all three.
          

        

        

          
          
        

      


      
what the runtime can be made to accept — enumerated here, with no key

      

        The declared arm is flat at zero, and it is worth being exact about why. Not
        because the model behaved: because for each of these invariants the space of
        declarations is small enough to walk, and every one that breaks the rule is
        refused while every one that does not is accepted. The rule refuses what it
        says it refuses, and nothing else.
      

      


      
the ladder

      

        Three invariants per rung. The prose arm is graded twice — by a lexical net
        that needs no key and under-counts, and by an extraction call that is a model
        grading a model. The declared arm reports two different things: what the model
        tried, and what the system let through.
